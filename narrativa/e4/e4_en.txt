Have you noticed how “working hard” has become a kind of medal.
Long hours.
A packed schedule.
Exhaustion at the end of the day.
And yet, a strange feeling.
The feeling that you truly gave it your all, but barely moved forward.
The point is, there’s a very common confusion.
Working hard is not the same as working right.
And that difference isn’t motivational.
It’s mechanical.
Working hard is intensity.
Working right is direction.
When the direction is wrong, intensity only increases friction.
And the most guaranteed outcome isn’t progress.
It’s fatigue.
The proof shows up in a place most people don’t even classify as work.
Your attention.
Researcher Gloria Mark has measured how people shift focus across screens, and in more recent work she reports averages in the range of dozens of seconds per screen before switching. In one of these findings, the number cited is 47 seconds.
Now do the math carefully.
An hour has 3,600 seconds.
If the switch happens, on average, every 47 seconds, that’s more than 70 focus shifts in a single hour.
Even if you’re working hard, you’re working in fragments.
And every fragment charges a fee.
This is the mechanism.
Task switching cost.
The American Psychological Association summarizes research on multitasking and cites estimates that this switching can consume a huge portion of productive time, reaching around 40 percent in some scenarios.
Forty percent sounds abstract until it becomes a clock.
In an 8-hour day, 40 percent is 3 hours and 12 minutes.
That’s not “laziness.”
That’s not “lack of grit.”
That’s time disappearing into mental recalibration between one thing and another.
And it gets worse because of a detail with a name.
Attention residue.
Researcher Sophie Leroy showed, in experiments, that when you switch from one task to another, part of your mind remains stuck on the previous task, and that reduces performance on the next task until you mentally close out the previous one.
Notice how this changes the entire conversation.
You can work hard all day.
And still be paying two invisible taxes, all the time.
The switching tax.
And the attention-residue tax.
And then comes the real cost.
Because the cost isn’t only fatigue.
It’s rework.
Small mistakes.
Mediocre decisions.
And a kind of slowness that doesn’t show up on your calendar, but shows up in your results.
Think about a simple, very common example.
A 30-minute meeting with 6 people.
That doesn’t cost 30 minutes.
It costs 3 total work hours combined.
If each person costs, say, 50 dollars per hour to the company, that meeting costs 150 dollars in direct time alone, not counting the time it takes to regain focus afterward. And regaining focus is exactly where switching cost starts charging.
There’s a second mechanism that makes many people work hard without realizing they’re working wrong.
Parkinson’s Law.
The idea, popularized by C. Northcote Parkinson in an essay published in The Economist in 1955, is that work tends to expand to fill the time available.
In practical terms, when you have “all day” to do something, that something often takes all day.
Not because it got better.
But because the shape of time sets the size of the work.
That’s why you can push harder and still produce almost the same.
You simply gave smaller tasks more room to grow.
And this is where the difference between working hard and working right becomes concrete.
Working hard increases volume.
Working right changes leverage.
And leverage almost always looks less dramatic than effort.
It looks like choice.
Cutting.
Priority.
Focus.
And sometimes an uncomfortable decision to stop doing things that “feel like work” but don’t change the outcome.
There’s a line often attributed to Peter Drucker that helps lock this in.
The difference between doing things right and doing the right things.
Some sources debate attribution for the exact phrasing, but the idea remains useful because it points directly to the difference between efficiency and direction.
Efficiency without direction just makes you faster on the wrong road.
And what makes this a revelation, not just “good advice,” is realizing what you’re actually losing.
Not a dramatic loss.
A silent one.
Three hours a day evaporating into switching.
Tasks growing to fill time.
Meetings multiplying cost.
And your best effort being used to sustain motion, not results.
Working hard is admirable.
But working right is transformative.
Because working right reduces the invisible taxes.
And when those taxes drop, the same effort starts producing more.
Not by magic.
By mechanism
